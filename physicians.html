Personal brand websites for physicians

Your record is real. What surfaces is not.

Thirty years of practice, board certification, published research, teaching appointments. Search your own name and the first result is a directory listing you never wrote, with a photograph you did not choose.

01The problem

Your professional record is scattered across pages you do not control.

A physician's life's work sits in places built for other purposes. PubMed holds the papers but not the person. The hospital bio is three sentences, written by someone in communications, last updated two employers ago. Insurance directories carry the name, an address, and a specialty code. Rating sites carry a star average drawn from a handful of reviews, most of them about parking and waiting times.

None of it is wrong, exactly. It is simply thin, and it is what everyone finds, because no page exists that tells the whole story.

And more people are looking than you think

  • Prospective patientsDeciding between you and two other names on an insurance list
  • Referring physiciansChecking subspecialty focus before sending a complex case
  • Journalists and producersLooking for a credentialed voice on deadline
  • Conference and committee chairsConfirming you are the right person to invite
  • AttorneysVetting expert witnesses long before they call
  • Recruiters and boardsBuilding a shortlist you were never told about

There is a newer reader too. When someone asks ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini who you are, the answer is assembled from whatever is indexed and public. A thin footprint produces a thin, sometimes wrong, answer, and no one tells you it happened.

02The action

One authoritative page, built from your actual record.

The work is research first and design second. Before anything is written, your record is assembled from primary sources, published papers, board certifications, fellowship and residency history, hospital and faculty appointments, trial involvement, editorial and society roles, press coverage.

How the engagement runs

  • Research. Your published and verifiable record is gathered and checked against primary sources, not aggregator listings.
  • Interview. One structured conversation for the things no database holds: why you chose the subspecialty, what you changed, what you want to be known for.
  • Positioning. We agree what the site should say about you, and to whom, before a word is written.
  • Writing. Biography, career history, achievements, publications and speaking, written in professional register, not brochure copy.
  • Build. A fast, mobile ready site on your own domain, structured so search engines and AI assistants can read who you are, what you practise, and where.
  • Connection. Linked to the profiles that already carry your name, so the record reads as one consistent professional identity.
  • Launch. Published on a domain you own and control.

Every credential, appointment and publication on the page is verified against a primary source before it is published, and every claim is one that can be substantiated. Physician advertising is governed by the FTC, by state medical boards and by professional ethics guidance, so the site is written to that standard from the start. A page that survives scrutiny is worth more than one that flatters.

03The result

The first thing people find is the thing you wrote.

What changes is structural, and you can verify it yourself the day it launches:

  • A page you own, on a domain you control, that presents your record in full, rather than a directory stub presenting a fragment of it.
  • A single authoritative source a referring physician, a journalist or a committee can read in ninety seconds and come away knowing what you actually do.
  • Your credentials, appointments and publications stated accurately in one place, in a form search engines and AI assistants can read and repeat correctly.
  • Something to send. A link that does the introduction for you, instead of a CV attachment and an apology for the hospital bio.
